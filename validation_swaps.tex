\title{\textbf{Which Prediction Errors Matter?}\\[0.4em]
\large Finite Covariate-Correction Paths for Downstream Regression}
\author{}
\date{October 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Correcting errors in machine-learned covariates can change a treatment-effect
estimate in ways that separate corrections do not predict. Each correction
changes the regression's adjustment for other variables, so its effect can
depend on which errors have already been corrected. Existing methods use
verified measurements to correct inference and guide validation. We study the
joint effects of finite corrections on a fitted coefficient. Our diagnostic
replaces sets of proxy covariates with verified values and compares the resulting
refits with the sum of isolated correction effects. Exact least-squares identities
locate the source of nonadditivity in changes to the regression's cross-product
matrix. Correction paths describe how the coefficient moves as more measurements
are corrected, and their dispersion describes how much the choice of correction
set matters. Established Shapley methods attribute the endpoint change.
Simulations illustrate these distinctions. Applications to housing and text-coded
incivility compare refits with three additive approximations: isolated corrections
track small random correction sets closely but misstate the full correction.
The resulting diagnostic measures both the consequences
of individual errors and the additional changes produced by correcting them
together.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Correcting a covariate changes the regression's adjustment for confounding.
Consequently, the effect of correcting one observation can depend on which other
observations have already been corrected. This matters when researchers use
machine-learning predictions as covariates: an assessment of errors one at a time
can misrepresent the change in a treatment coefficient when those errors are
corrected together. We study these joint correction effects and develop a
diagnostic that measures them through regression refits.

Predicted covariates allow researchers to incorporate information that would
otherwise be expensive to measure, including attributes coded from text and
variables missing from linked datasets. Their errors can distort coefficient
estimates and conventional standard errors \citep{fong2021,battaglia2024}.
Prediction accuracy does not summarize that distortion because it does not
record how errors relate to treatment, outcomes, and the other regressors.
When a predicted variable serves as a confounder, its measurement quality is
also distinct from the assumptions that identify a causal effect
\citep{egami2022}. The practical question is how the observed errors change the
coefficient a researcher wants to interpret.

Our diagnostic compares the effect of correcting a set of measurements with
the sum of the effects of correcting each measurement separately. All
observations remain in the regression, and the outcome and specification stay
fixed. Only the selected proxy covariates are replaced with verified values.
The discrepancy between the joint and summed effects measures nonadditivity
for that set. Repeating the comparison across sets reveals which corrections
have effects that depend on the surrounding measurements. A correction path
summarizes the coefficient as the number of corrected measurements increases;
its dispersion records how different choices at the same correction budget
produce different results.

For ordinary least squares, the source of this dependence is explicit.
Disjoint corrections have additive effects on the estimating equation evaluated
at the proxy fit, but they also change the cross-product matrix that maps those
changes into coefficient movements. Its inverse depends on the whole correction
set. Thus even exact individual refits need not add up to the joint refit.
An algebraic example gives a jointly corrected coefficient of $1/2$ where
summing the isolated effects predicts zero
(Appendix~\ref{app:undetected-interactions}). This is a property of finite
regression corrections, established without simulation. Numerical examples then
illustrate how the discrepancy varies with error magnitude and its relation to
treatment.

Verified measurements already support methods for correcting inference with
predicted variables. These include methods for predicted outcomes,
prediction-powered inference and its efficiency refinements, and design-based
supervised learning \citep{wang2020,angelopoulos2023,angelopoulos2023ppi,egami2023}.
Extensions explicitly accommodate imputed covariates and nonuniform verification
samples \citep{kluger2025}. These procedures address estimation of a population
parameter. We examine how finite replacements change a specified fitted
coefficient, conditional on the available verified measurements. A partially
corrected fit is a diagnostic comparison; it does not inherit the inferential
properties of a bias-corrected estimator.

Target-specific verification also has a substantial literature. Two-phase
sampling allocates expensive measurements using information collected on a
larger sample \citep{neyman1938,amorim2021}. For regression and causal parameters,
allocation may depend on influence functions or on residual influence after
using auxiliary information \citep{chenlumley2022,wang2023design}. Active
statistical inference projects gold-minus-proxy score discrepancies through an
inverse Hessian, while adaptive multiwave M-estimation uses influence
contrasts to guide subsequent verification \citep{zrnic2024,kluger2026}.
Those methods already ask which errors matter for a target. Our comparison
asks how well additive summaries describe the consequences of correcting
specific sets. A discrepancy from a fixed influence approximation does not
establish a failure of an adaptive procedure that updates its scores or uses
different gold and proxy fits.

Local influence analysis studies how measurement error affects regression
estimates \citep{lee1996}. Simulation-extrapolation adds noise and extrapolates
toward the error-free case \citep{cook1994}. Infinitesimal-jackknife methods provide refit
approximations and error bounds, including higher-order expansions
\citep{giordano2019higher,giordano2019}. Influence functions and Shapley values
also explain predictions and value training observations
\citep{koh2017,ghorbani2019,karlas2024}. We apply baseline-replacement Shapley
values to a regression coefficient \citep{sundararajan2020}. The mean path
under independent random corrections is Owen's multilinear extension evaluated
at a common inclusion probability \citep{owen1972}. These tools summarize and
attribute finite coefficient changes; the regression diagnostic supplies their
interpretation here.

The analysis separates three quantities: the endpoint coefficient change,
nonadditivity of corrections, and dispersion across correction sets. They
answer different questions. In particular, a straight mean path need not imply
that the choice of corrections is inconsequential. We report all three and
compare full refits with isolated corrections and two influence approximations.
The numerical examples hold prediction RMSE fixed across error patterns to
illustrate how error alignment and concentration affect these quantities.
Two replication archives then show how the comparisons behave with actual
predictions and reference measurements. In housing, we correct adjustment
covariates; in a text application, we correct the regressor of interest. Both
applications concern descriptive associations, and neither evaluates a
prospective sampling policy.

\section{A game of verified replacements}

Let $X$ be the proxy design matrix, including an intercept, and $y$ the outcome.
Let $G_1,\ldots,G_m$ be disjoint sets of rows with verified design values.
A player is one such correction unit; it can be a single observation or a batch.
For $S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}$, replace precisely the rows belonging to players
in $S$, giving $X_S$. Define the value of that correction set as
\begin{equation}
 v(S)=e_t^\top(X_S^\top X_S)^{-1}X_S^\top y,
 \label{eq:game}
\end{equation}
where $t$ indexes the coefficient of interest. Every design used in this game
must have full column rank. We use least-squares refits and report a failure if a
coalition is rank deficient; a pseudoinverse would silently redefine the target.

The baseline $v(\varnothing)$ is the proxy fit. The endpoint $v(M)$ corrects the
declared pool $M$, not any unverified observations outside it. Both are
finite-sample regression coefficients. Even if the gold regression identifies a
causal parameter in the population, their difference is not itself an estimate
of population measurement-error bias without additional assumptions.

For a given correction set, define its departure from isolated corrections as
\begin{equation}
 R(S)=v(S)-v(\varnothing)
       -\sum_{i\in S}\{v(\{i\})-v(\varnothing)\}.
 \label{eq:nonadditivity}
\end{equation}
This discrepancy is in coefficient units. The correction game is additive
exactly when $R(S)=0$ for every set $S$. A zero discrepancy for one set alone
does not establish additivity, since interactions within it can cancel.

\subsection{Mean paths and correction-set dispersion}

Independently include player $i$ with probability $p_i$. The expected coefficient
is the multilinear extension of the finite game \citep{owen1972}:
\begin{equation}
 F(\mathbf p)=\sum_{S\subseteq M}v(S)
       \prod_{i\in S}p_i\prod_{i\notin S}(1-p_i).
\end{equation}
The common-probability path is $\phi(p)=F(p,\ldots,p)$. Let $S_k$ be uniform over
all subsets of exactly $k$ players and $\mu_k=\E[v(S_k)]$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \phi(p)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}p^k(1-p)^{m-k}\mu_k.
 \label{eq:mixture}
\end{equation}
The Bernoulli design has a random correction count; the fixed-budget design does
not. Their variances differ because the former also varies that count. We
report both, and store the entire finite distribution at each fixed budget.
These are distributions over correction sets conditional on the data, not
sampling distributions of a population estimator.

If $v(S)=v(\varnothing)+\sum_{i\in S}a_i$, then both mean paths interpolate
between the endpoints. Curvature therefore rules out additivity, but a straight
path does not establish it: average interactions can cancel. A straight path
also need not have small dispersion. Accordingly, we report errors of additive
approximations at the coalition level as well as mean curvature and fixed-budget
dispersion.

\subsection{Attribution along the path}

Let $S_{-i}(p)$ independently include every player other than $i$ with probability
$p$, and define
\begin{equation}
 d_i(p)=\E[v(S_{-i}(p)\cup\{i\})-v(S_{-i}(p))].
\end{equation}
The derivative satisfies $\phi'(p)=\sum_i d_i(p)$. The Shapley attribution is
\begin{align}
 s_i&=\int_0^1 d_i(p)\,dp\\
 &=\frac1m\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}
 \E[v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)\mid |S|=k,\ i\notin S],
 \label{eq:shapley}
\end{align}
so $\sum_i s_i=v(M)-v(\varnothing)$. This integral representation is established
\citep{owen1972,okhrati2021}. Uniformly averaging over all subsets of other
players instead gives $d_i(1/2)$, the unnormalized Banzhaf attribution, which
generally does not sum to the endpoint change. The implementation distinguishes
the two; its tests use a three-player unanimity game, for which the respective
per-player values are $1/3$ and $1/4$.

The choice of game matters \citep{sundararajan2020}. These values attribute
movement in a regression coefficient, not prediction accuracy or social welfare.
Positive contributions are not inherently beneficial, since improvement depends
on a separate target and loss. Batch-game values also depend on grouping and
generally do not equal sums of individual-row Shapley values.

\section{What an influence approximation omits}

Write $\widehat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$, $r=y-X\widehat\beta$, and
$A=X^\top X$. For a coalition's design change $\Delta=X_S-X$, define
\begin{align}
 B_S&=X^\top\Delta+\Delta^\top X+\Delta^\top\Delta,\\
 q_S&=\Delta^\top r-X^\top\Delta\widehat\beta
                   -\Delta^\top\Delta\widehat\beta.
\end{align}
Subtracting the old normal equations from the new ones gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\beta_S-\widehat\beta=(A+B_S)^{-1}q_S.
 \label{eq:update}
\end{equation}
This is ordinary least-squares algebra, not a new influence-function result.
For disjoint row corrections, $q_S$ is the sum of the individual paired-score
changes. Dependence of the inverse matrix on $S$ can still make their coefficient
effects nonadditive.

We compare the exact target change with three additive baselines on the same
correction sets. The principal influence baseline is $e_t^\top A^{-1}q_S$: it
retains the finite quadratic score term while holding the inverse Gram matrix
fixed. A feature-amplitude derivative additionally drops
$-\Delta^\top\Delta\widehat\beta$. Finally, the single-repair baseline sums
$v(\{i\})-v(\varnothing)$ over the chosen players. It allows each isolated repair
to change the inverse matrix, but ignores interactions among repairs.

Neither influence approximation uniformly dominates the other. Both generally
have second-order error as feature-error amplitude goes to zero. Retaining one
quadratic term does not provide a complete second-order expansion. A large
failure of the paired-score baseline does not prove that established active
inference procedures fail: they target sampling efficiency and valid inference,
and may update their fitted scores or use different gold and proxy influence
functions. Our comparison addresses a fixed-baseline approximation to the finite
raw-replacement game.

\section{Numerical illustrations}

To illustrate the algebraic mechanism, we simulate independent standard Normal variables $U,W,\eta,\epsilon$ and set
\begin{equation}
 D=\mathbf 1\{0.8U+0.3W+\eta>0\},\qquad
 Y=0.5D+U+0.3W+0.5\epsilon.
\end{equation}
The verified design is $(1,D,U,W)$, and the proxy replaces $U$ by $U+e$.
Conditional exogeneity and the additive outcome model identify the population
treatment coefficient $0.5$ using the verified covariates. The realized gold
coefficient need not equal $0.5$; our comparisons always use the realized fit.

The main example uses seed 2024, 96 observations, eight randomly assigned equal
batches, and proxy RMSE $0.75$. Within the same dataset we compare independent
diffuse errors, errors supported on the first batch only, and errors proportional
to centered treatment. Each error vector is rescaled to have the same RMSE.
The one-active-batch scenario is deliberately an additive control. The aligned
scenario is a stress test of differential measurement error, not a claim about
its prevalence in trained ML systems. The benchmark prescribes errors rather
than training a predictor. In the aligned case the proxy confounder adds a
multiple of treatment, so the proxy and gold designs span the same space while
assigning different coefficients to treatment. Partial row replacement changes
that relation, making this a transparent constructed stress test.

We enumerate all $2^8$ correction sets. Coalition approximation RMSE weights each
set equally, equivalently using a Bernoulli design with $p=1/2$. Mean curvature
is the maximum absolute departure of $\phi(p)$ from endpoint interpolation on a
101-point grid. We also report the SD at a fixed half-pool budget. The repeated
benchmark uses seeds 0--19 and error RMSE values $0.05,0.25,0.75,1.5$, holding each
seed's dataset, batches and error direction fixed as amplitude changes. Its
\CovariateReplications\ designs were specified in the analysis plan before this
benchmark was run; the overall project remains exploratory.

The repeated benchmark shows why a useful diagnostic must include the baselines
rather than presume their failure. Approximation error generally shrinks with
error amplitude, and the feature derivative can outperform the finite paired
score. No single approximation is uniformly best across the prescribed designs.
Every coalition in all \CovariateReplications\ designs has full rank. The largest
design-matrix condition number is \CovariateMaxCondition; these findings therefore
do not require near-singular matrices in this benchmark. This does not establish
robustness to arbitrary conditioning or superiority in other data-generating
processes.

\section{Evidence from two replication archives}
\label{sec:archives}

We examine two archives selected because they provide paired predictions and
reference measurements. The first is the housing dataset distributed with
\citet{kluger2025}: 46,418 observations with measured income and housing prices,
and both predicted and reference values for nightlights and road length.
We regress housing price on an intercept, income, nightlights, and road length,
using the archive's transformations. The target is the income coefficient per
one standard deviation of income. A correction replaces both adjustment
covariates for one row. Fixed centering and scaling improve numerical stability
without changing the regression specification.

The second is the Reddit application of \citet{fong2021}. We reproduce the
archive's weighted support-vector classifier using its original 2,413-row
training CSV and document-term matrix, then predict incivility for the separate
613-row validation sample. No validation outcomes or labels enter classifier
training or tuning. In that validation sample, we regress comment score,
clamped to $[-10,10]$ as in the original application, on an intercept and
incivility. Here a correction replaces the regressor of interest with its human
label. This is a diagnostic of the validation sample, not a reproduction of the
original paper's primary-sample estimate. Both targets are descriptive
associations; neither comparison identifies a causal effect.

Each row is a correction unit, including rows whose proxy already agrees with
the reference. At seven budgets from 1\% to 100\% of rows, we compare direct
least-squares refits with the same three additive approximations used above.
For each interior budget, 200 random permutation prefixes give uniformly
sampled correction sets; the full-correction endpoint is computed once.
The comparisons therefore concern random corrections within these observed
pools, not a policy for discovering previously unobserved errors.

the endpoint gap nor the error of the feature derivative alone describes the
diagnostic. These exploratory results show that the joint effects are measurable
in actual prediction archives. They do not establish their prevalence across
applications or that accounting for them changes a verification decision.

\section{Discussion}

With a fully verified benchmark or pilot pool, the method can reveal which
correction budgets and sets leave a coefficient sensitive, whether influence
approximations are adequate, and which declared units account for the endpoint
change. This can inform hypotheses about a later validation design. It cannot
by itself price the unseen errors of a new population. Learning an acquisition
policy would require a separate pilot-to-target design, available sampling
features, an objective such as estimation error or tail risk, and comparison
with established influence-based allocation under a common label budget.

An exact counterexample in Appendix~\ref{app:undetected-interactions} makes the
information limit concrete. Two possible completions of the unverified
covariates give identical observed data whenever a random pilot misses two
specific rows. One completion has no measurement error; the other has interacting
corrections that an additive summary misrepresents. The probability of missing
those rows is known from the sampling design. Thus an uneventful pilot alone
cannot certify negligible interactions outside it. This is an indistinguishability
argument, not a claim about how frequently such configurations occur in practice.

A prospective study must also distinguish choosing which measurements to
verify from choosing which predictions to use. Filtered prediction-powered
inference selects regions for prediction-based augmentation while retaining the
full-population target \citep{xu2026}. OPAL optimizes labeling policies for
prediction-assisted inference using influence-function information
\citep{ma2026}. Power analysis for prediction-powered inference addresses labeled
sample size through asymptotic variance and power calculations \citep{chen2026}.
These are relevant comparators for a future decision rule. Our conditional
correction distributions do not establish better allocation, a smaller required
sample, or valid selection of a low-distortion population.

Exact enumeration is exponential and is capped at 16 players in the implementation.
For larger games, the code provides permutation Shapley estimates with numerical
Monte Carlo standard errors. Each sampled order telescopes to the endpoint gap.
That estimator does not recover the entire correction-set distribution. The
archive applications instead sample fixed-size sets directly to estimate means
and dispersion; rare-tail estimation would require further computation. Large batches change
the question, so grouping is part of the diagnostic specification, not an
innocuous speed optimization.

The present evidence supports a finite-sample diagnostic application. A stronger
research contribution would require evidence that the path changes a
consequential decision, or a prospective validation study showing better
estimation under the same acquisition budget. The archive applications measure
approximation discrepancies, but do not establish either decision benefit.

\section{Linear outcome corrections as a boundary case}

For a fixed linear treatment contrast, let $\psi_g$ be the change from correcting
cluster $g$, and $\widehat\tau_P$ the proxy contrast. Raw replacement gives
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\tau_R(S_k)=\widehat\tau_P+\sum_{g\in S_k}\psi_g,\qquad
 \E\widehat\tau_R(S_k)=\widehat\tau_P+
 \frac{k}{G}(\widehat\tau_G-\widehat\tau_P).
\end{equation}
The mean is mechanically linear, but the correction-set distribution need not be
concentrated or approximately Normal. Finite-population limit theory provides

and predictive $R^2$ fixed across three patterns. Its diffuse and concentrated
patterns also share both endpoints, while their dispersions differ.

\section{A validation sample can miss interacting errors}
\label{app:undetected-interactions}

Consider a finite cohort of $N\geq 3$ observations. The outcome $Y$, treatment
$D$, and proxy covariate $\widetilde U$ are observed for everyone. Only a simple
random sample of $n$ distinct rows receives verification. The regression includes
an intercept, $D$, and the covariate. Fix the observed data and two possible
completions of its unverified values as follows:
\begin{table}[H]\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
Rows & $D$ & $Y$ & $\widetilde U$ & $U^{(0)}$ & $U^{(1)}$ \\
\midrule
$N-2$ ordinary rows & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
Row $i$ & 1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\
Row $j$ & 0 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 2 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Observed data and two possible covariate completions. Only the verified
values of the pilot rows are observed. The last two columns agree on every
ordinary row. Values are specified analytically.}
\end{table}
These are stipulated values defining an algebraic example, not simulation
output. Completion 0 has no measurement error. In completion 1 only rows $i$
and $j$ require correction. Write $v(S)$ for the coefficient on $D$ after
correcting rows in $S$. Every possible correction design has full column rank.
Indeed, the ordinary rows imply a fitted intercept of zero, row $j$ gives the
covariate coefficient $1/u_j$, and row $i$ gives
$v(S)=1-u_i/u_j$, where $u_i\in\{0,1\}$ and $u_j\in\{1,2\}$ are the covariate
values used in that fit. All outcomes are fitted exactly. Hence, in completion 1,
\begin{equation}
 v(\varnothing)=1,\qquad v(\{i\})=0,\qquad
 v(\{j\})=1,\qquad v(\{i,j\})=\tfrac12.
 \label{eq:hidden-example}
\end{equation}
The interaction is
\begin{equation}
 v(\{i,j\})-v(\{i\})-v(\{j\})+v(\varnothing)=\tfrac12.
\end{equation}
Adding the isolated correction effects predicts a jointly corrected coefficient
of zero; the actual coefficient is $1/2$. The two calculations therefore fall
on opposite sides of any substantive threshold strictly between zero and
$1/2$. This concerns a regression coefficient; the example makes no claim of
causal identification.

Let $E$ be the event that the pilot misses both special rows. For $n\leq N-2$,
\begin{equation}
 q=\Pr(E)=\frac{\binom{N-2}{n}}{\binom{N}{n}}
          =\frac{(N-n)(N-n-1)}{N(N-1)}.
 \label{eq:pilot-miss}
\end{equation}
For $n>N-2$, $q=0$. On $E$, the entire observed record, including the full cohort's
outcomes and proxies, the sampled identities, and their verified values, is
identical in the two completions. Every correction available in the pilot is
zero, although completion 1 has the interaction in
\eqref{eq:hidden-example}. For example, $N=100$ and $n=10$ give
$q=89/110$, about $81\%$.

This also bounds any procedure using these observations. Let $T\in\{0,1\}$
flag consequential interactions, and suppose its false-positive probability in
completion 0 is at most $\alpha$. Allow the procedure its own randomization.
The sampling law and observed data agree on $E$, so
\begin{align}
 \Pr_1(T=1)
 &=\Pr_1(T=1,E)+\Pr_1(T=1,E^c)\\
 &\leq\Pr_0(T=1,E)+1-q\leq\alpha+1-q.
 \label{eq:pilot-power-bound}
\end{align}
Thus its probability of failing to flag completion 1 is at least $q-\alpha$
when that quantity is positive. This elementary indistinguishability argument
rules out a uniform guarantee over unrestricted covariate completions under
the stated random-pilot design.

The example deliberately concentrates identifying variation in two observable
rows. A design that targets those rows can avoid the problem; this is not a
lower bound for every adaptive sampling policy. Nor does the calculation
establish the prevalence of hidden interactions. It shows precisely why a
random pilot's absence of errors is insufficient on its own: extrapolation to
unverified rows needs restrictions on the possible errors, a sampling design
that covers consequential rows, or other information linking observed and
unobserved corrections.

\section{Reproducibility and checks}

The repository contains the analysis plan, complete coalition values for the main
covariate examples, repeated-design summaries, generated tables and figures, and
separate metadata for the benchmarks. Tests check the normal-equation identity
against independent least-squares fits, exact Shapley against every permutation
in a small game, its agreement with integrated marginal paths, partial-validation
endpoints, the additive control, small-amplitude behavior, and explicit failures
for rank-deficient coalitions. The historical implementation audit is retained in
the research notes; its domain-selection and calibration claims are not used as
evidence here.

The archive pilot fixes source-file checksums and uses the housing repository at
commit \texttt{f4adf2d} and the Fong--Tyler Dataverse deposit, version 1.2.\footnote{
Housing: \url{https://github.com/DanKluger/Predict-Then-Debias_Bootstrap}.
Reddit: \url{https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/QQHBHY}. The machine-readable source
manifest contains the full commit identifier and file-specific download URLs.}
The original Reddit training CSV has 2,413 rows, whereas Dataverse's converted
tabular file has 2,412. The document-term indices match the original CSV, which
we use without reordering. Training and validation comment IDs do not overlap;
the archive's matrix-to-row ordering is retained. Human labels and other
reference measurements are treated as fixed, not assumed to measure constructs
without error.

The empirical specifications and the correction of the training-row count are
recorded in the analysis plan. No downstream coefficients were inspected before
specifying the two targets and budgets; this is a self-attested exploratory
plan, not a preregistration. Saved outputs include every sampled-set coefficient,
approximation errors, conditioning, numerical Monte Carlo standard errors for
means and RMSE, and software versions. All sampled designs have full rank.
Individual-row updates computed from sufficient statistics agree with independent
least-squares refits on random, high-leverage, and large-update checks. Neither
archive supplies the design needed here to interpret HC0 scales as valid
population uncertainty; they are used only to put discrepancies on a common
descriptive scale.
