**The question is consequential, and there is direct prior work on it:** identify errors in ML-produced measurements that matter for a downstream coefficient or causal effect, then use that information to allocate expensive verification. The appropriate comparison is two-phase inference with error-prone covariates, treatments, or confounders. Influence on an ML model's training predictions answers a different question; generic prediction-powered confidence intervals do not exhaust this one. The closest papers already combine downstream influence discrepancies with adaptive verification. The current repository demonstrates a simpler, additive outcome-correction diagnostic. A broader contribution remains possible, but its novelty must concern a specific failure of existing methods or a useful new decision guarantee. **Decision: narrow and test; novelty of the proposed extension remains unresolved.**

**The closest paper explicitly asks which proxy errors matter for the target.** Kluger and Bates's 2026 preprint, *M-estimation under Two-Phase Multiwave Sampling with Applications to Prediction-Powered Inference*, defines a target-coordinate gold-minus-proxy influence discrepancy in equation (15). Equations (16)–(17) use its conditional squared magnitude for approximately greedy verification across waves; §4.2 learns the necessary moments from verified observations. Gold and proxy influence contributions have their own parameter limits and Hessians. The allocation result assumes identity tuning and prespecified wave weights; §4.3 separately optimizes matrix tuning for a fixed design. This is direct overlap with the proposed broad idea, although it is not a joint optimum over tuning, all waves, and total budget. Its convex M-estimation theory does not automatically cover every causal estimator with learned nuisance functions. ([Kluger and Bates, §§3–4](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16933v1))

Zrnic and Candès's published ICML 2024 paper, *Active Statistical Inference*, is also closer than the earlier discussion suggested. Its corrected loss combines proxy losses with inverse-probability-weighted gold-minus-proxy losses. Section 7.1 optimizes asymptotic variance for a chosen parameter coordinate using score errors projected through the inverse Hessian; §7.2 estimates that quantity and stabilizes sampling. The GLM examples predict outcomes, but the general loss permits a costly covariate: let the observed record contain the outcome and cheap regressors, and let the paper's expensive “label” be the missing true covariate. That reduction is our interpretation of the general formulation, not a separately demonstrated causal application. Erroneous regressors can also make a corrected finite-sample loss indefinite, so consistency and existence of a suitable solution require attention. ([ASI, §§5–7, arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.03208v2); [conference record](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/zrnic24a.html))

There is an explicit causal precedent. Wang, Wang, and Miao's *A Maximin Optimal Approach for Sampling Designs in Two-phase Studies*, accepted by *Statistica Sinica*, derives allocation from the conditional variance of the full-data efficient influence function. Supplement Example S3 treats an ATE with expensive confounders; Example S2 treats expensive regression covariates. Its pilot estimates unknown influence moments. Crucially, supplement §S3 leaves a rigorous analysis of its proposed estimator with reused, dependently sampled pilot data open. The population allocation result is established; a universal adaptive implementation theorem is not. ([Main paper, Lemma 1 and Theorem 1](https://www3.stat.sinica.edu.tw/preprint/SS-2024-0359_Preprint.pdf); [supplement, Examples S2–S3 and §S3](https://www3.stat.sinica.edu.tw/preprint/supp/2024-0359_supp.pdf); [publication status](https://www3.stat.sinica.edu.tw/statistica/fp/SS-2024-0359.html))

**“Which errors matter?” has three different meanings.** The signed displacement of the proxy-only estimate tells us how measurement errors distort a reported result. The variability of a corrected estimator tells us where additional verification improves precision. The finite change from replacing selected records and refitting tells us what a particular repair accomplishes. These are related, but optimizing one does not optimize the others. Opposing errors can cancel in the aggregate displacement while generating considerable verification variance. A large, predictable correction can be handled well by augmentation, leaving relatively little uncertainty to resolve through further labels.

Here is the relevant technology in a common notation. Let \(V\) contain cheap measurements and proxies, \(U\) the expensive true variable, and \(\widetilde U\) its proxy. For a smooth estimating equation with score \(s\), derivative matrix \(A\), and target coordinate \(j\), a useful **target influence discrepancy** is

\[
 d_j(V,U)=-e_j^\top A^{-1}
 \{s(V,U;\theta)-s(V,\widetilde U;\theta)\}.
\]

This is an explanatory specialization, not a new formula. Evaluated at the proxy fit, its sample contribution approximates a small replacement of probability mass from a proxy record to its verified counterpart. Evaluated at the gold target with the appropriate derivative, it instead enters a corrected estimator's expansion. The paired score retains the entire record-level measurement change; it need not differentiate an infinitesimal feature error. Neither evaluation is automatically the separate-gold/proxy-Hessian contrast in Kluger–Bates.

For allocation, suppose \(\phi(V,U)\) is a valid scalar full-data influence function, \(g(V)\) is a fixed augmentation, and verification occurs independently given \(V\), with known probability \(\pi(V)>0\). The augmented contribution is \(g(V)+R\{\phi(V,U)-g(V)\}/\pi(V)\). Conditional variance algebra gives the policy-dependent variance term \(E[a_g(V)/\pi(V)]\), where \(a_g(V)=E[(\phi-g)^2\mid V]\). At expected cost budget \(b\), the corresponding allocation is

\[
 \pi^*(V)=\min\!\left\{1,\max\!\left\{\varepsilon,
 \sqrt{\frac{a_g(V)}{\lambda c(V)}}\right\}\right\},
 \qquad E[c(V)\pi^*(V)]=b.
\]

Here \(c(V)>0\) is verification cost, \(\varepsilon>0\) is an imposed exploration floor, and \(\lambda\) enforces a feasible budget. With optimal augmentation \(g(V)=E[\phi\mid V]\), the relevant second moment becomes \(\operatorname{Var}(\phi\mid V)\). With a fixed proxy correction, it also contains squared conditional error relative to that proxy. Thus **raw prediction accuracy or classifier confidence is not the allocation criterion**. The criterion depends on the target, correction estimator, and information already used. This derivation assumes the stated influence expansion and sampling scheme; fixed-size or clustered sampling needs the appropriate design calculation.

This distinction already appears in Chen and Lumley's 2022 work: optimal stratified IPW allocation uses target influence variability, whereas generalized raking uses residual influences after exploiting phase-one information. More recently, Ma and Candès's June 2026 OPAL preprint learns policies for general efficient-influence-function targets. Its generality relies on a negligible remainder assumption; it does not prove nuisance-estimation conditions for every causal application. ([Chen and Lumley, §§4.2–4.3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8918008/); [OPAL, §2.2 and Appendix G](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.03211))

**Covariate replacement changes the mathematical problem in this repository.** Its [manuscript](../validation_swaps.tex), equations `eq:add`, `eq:raw`, and `eq:ht`, and [implementation](../replicate_final.py) enumerate additive outcome corrections with fixed estimator weights. Even ordinary least squares loses that additivity when the corrected variable is a regressor. Let \(X\) be the proxy design, \(\widehat\beta\) its OLS fit, \(r=y-X\widehat\beta\), and \(\Delta\) the selected covariate corrections. Direct subtraction of the normal equations gives, assuming full column rank,

\[
 \widehat\beta(X+\Delta)-\widehat\beta(X)
 =\big[X^\top X+B\big]^{-1}d,
\]

where \(B=X^\top\Delta+\Delta^\top X+\Delta^\top\Delta\) and \(d=\Delta^\top r-X^\top\Delta\widehat\beta-\Delta^\top\Delta\widehat\beta\). This is our elementary algebra, not a novelty claim. A paired-score approximation freezes the inverse matrix at \((X^\top X)^{-1}\) but retains all of \(d\). A derivative in feature amplitude additionally drops the quadratic term. Because the inverse matrix changes, summing exact single-record replacements does not generally recover a simultaneous replacement.

The [local verifier](<../research_notes/ML measurement errors and inference/verify_covariate_substitution.py>) checks this identity against full refits in **100 randomized cases**, with maximum discrepancy **\(3.24\times10^{-15}\)**. In its deterministic example, one two-record correction changes the treatment coefficient by **0.137714**; the sum of separate exact corrections is **0.104446**, and the paired-score approximation is **0.163946**. These are constructed mechanism checks, not performance evidence. The paired-score approximation is excellent for sufficiently small corrections in the same example. A convincing comparison must therefore use it, not only the weaker feature derivative.

Finite and higher-order reweighting approximations already have error-bound theory in the infinitesimal-jackknife literature. Non-Normal tails for finite-population sampling also have established approximation theory. Showing interaction, adding a second-order term, or demonstrating a bad Normal approximation is insufficient by itself to establish a new method. ([Giordano et al., Theorem 1](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v89/giordano19a/giordano19a.pdf); [higher-order companion](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.12116); [Hu, Robinson, and Wang, Theorems 2.1–2.2](https://www.numdam.org/item/10.1051/ps/2010027.pdf))

**Budget choice requires stating which uncertainty must shrink.** At fixed cohort size, a pilot can estimate a precision-versus-verification-budget curve; invert it to choose a budget for a desired standard error or interval width. Include pilot costs and uncertainty in the fitted curve. More cohort observations and more verified observations are different investments, with potentially different variance contributions. The closest methods generally optimize a specified sampling fraction or wave budget; that does not establish a jointly optimal choice of cohort size, verification budget, and stopping rule. Conversely, translating variance into sample size is not automatically a new contribution.

If the objective is a finite-budget probability of a consequential error, define it explicitly: for example, the probability that the corrected estimate differs from the fully verified *sample estimate* by more than a substantive tolerance. That differs from error relative to a population causal effect, which also includes cohort sampling uncertainty. A raw partial refit estimates a mixed-measurement result; it need not consistently estimate the all-gold target. Inverse-probability-corrected estimating equations address that target under additional assumptions, but an unbiased score does not make its nonlinear root exactly unbiased.

For causal inference, specify whether ML imputes an actual confounder or treatment, or estimates nuisance functions such as propensity scores. Orthogonality to nuisance estimation does not generally remove confounder measurement error. An ATE additionally requires the relevant identification and overlap assumptions. Verified labels cannot supply those assumptions. A fixed-nuisance score diagnostic and a full nuisance-refitting diagnostic answer different questions. This is a reason to start with one identified causal setting, not a reason to abandon the question.

**The surviving research opportunity must improve an implementable decision.** Kluger et al.'s 2025 preprint already covers imputed covariates and nonuniform sampling, including discussion of clustered designs. Clusters are therefore neither a necessary setting for this project nor an automatic source of novelty. ([*Prediction-Powered Inference with Imputed Covariates and Nonuniform Sampling*, §§2–4](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.18577v2)) A defensible candidate claim is narrower: under stated stability and measurement-error assumptions, accounting for finite replacement interactions improves verification-budget decisions for a consequential-error tolerance beyond strong influence-based designs. Neither this search nor the existing repository establishes that claim.

The smallest discriminating experiment uses a paired proxy/gold dataset with a meaningful downstream treatment coefficient and a prespecified causal interpretation. Hide gold values outside a randomized pilot and sequentially selected verification samples. Compare uniform allocation, ASI-style projected-score allocation, feasible Kluger–Bates multiwave allocation, and efficient residual-influence allocation. Include a robust active policy: Li, Zrnic, and Candès's published NeurIPS 2025 method protects against misspecified error models through budget-preserving mixtures and minimax fitting, with asymptotic rather than unconditional finite-sample guarantees. ([*Robust Sampling for Active Statistical Inference*, §§2–3](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.08991v1)) Compare a proposed finite-correction rule only after its deployable algorithm is specified.

Use equal total labeling costs, repeated samples, held-out evaluation, and prespecified tolerances. Measure tail error, interval coverage, budget required, and the gap between influence approximations and full refits; distinguish measurement error against the all-gold fit from causal estimation error. Fully labeled oracle designs diagnose headroom but are not deployable competitors. Stop the method-paper path if the rule reduces to existing machinery without a substantive result, if full refits rarely change decisions, or if gains disappear against strong baselines after pilot costs. Continue only if a repeatable improvement survives in a prospectively identifiable setting, or a new theorem explains a consequential limitation.

A small pilot can miss the observations carrying the largest errors. Without bounds or predictive structure, populations agreeing on every observed value can have arbitrarily different unseen corrections; no influence formula identifies those corrections. Any claimed tail certificate must account for error discovery and estimated variance, not merely substitute a noisy pilot variance into a population bound. Rare-tail design and adaptive sample-size choice are not assumed unstudied here. The search did not settle novelty for their particular combination with finite covariate replacement.

The current repository can remain useful as a diagnostic and teaching implementation; renaming its sampling distribution “validation swaps” supplies no novelty. The proposed broader research deserves a focused test because finite corrections and learning the allocation can change practical decisions. Its contribution must be demonstrated against the direct literature above.
